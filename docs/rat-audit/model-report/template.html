Boston rodent report forecast

Two models built from 3 years of Boston 311 data. The forecast predicts how many rodent reports each 250 m block will get each week. The recurrence model predicts whether reports come back to an address within 60 days after the city closes a case. Both predict reports people file, not counts of rats.

    How the forecast works

    A gradient-boosted model: hundreds of small decision trees, each learning a rule such as "blocks with many recent rodent reports and many trash complaints get more reports next week", each new tree fixing the previous ones' mistakes.

    Inputs: recent rodent reports here and next door; dead animal, indoor pest, trash and dumping reports; code-enforcement tickets; building count, age and units; season; the Sept 1 move-out; temperature. Rain, snow, restaurants, demolitions and events were tested and add nothing.

    Which factors matter most

    Shuffle one factor across blocks and weeks, keep everything else, and measure how much worse the forecast gets. A bigger bar means the model leans on that factor more. Bars show the loss in fit (Poisson deviance); the number on the right is how many points of the top-10% capture are lost. The final model was trained on these weeks, so this shows what it relies on; the out-of-sample accuracy is in the charts above.

    The two biggest factors describe where people file 311 reports at all and how dense the housing is, not rodent activity itself. Rodent reports here and next door come third and fourth. Part of what the model learns is who reports, which is why it forecasts reports and not rats.

      Partial dependence: take 20,000 recent block-weeks, set one factor to each value on the x-axis, keep the rest as they were, and average the forecast. The y-axis is expected rodent reports per block per week. These curves describe the model, not cause and effect.
