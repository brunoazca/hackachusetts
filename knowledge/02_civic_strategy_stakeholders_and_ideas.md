# Hackachusetts 2026 — Civic strategy, stakeholders, and ideas

Research checked: **October 24, 2026**, Eastern. Read alongside the [rules playbook](01_rules_judging_and_event_playbook.md) and [winner analysis](03_past_winners_and_patterns.md).

## 1. Recommended strategic position

Build **one useful Massachusetts civic workflow with authoritative evidence, accessible interaction, a clear human decision point, and demonstrable Kiro development**. Let a judge watch a person finish a task. A broad chatbot, a map without a next action, or an unsupported claim of citywide transformation will be harder to defend.

**Updated after the deeper public-data and public-voices research:** consider **Last 100 Feet** (journey barriers and repair scenarios), **ShiftFits** (program/schedule feasibility), or **OpenDoor** (conflicting service instructions and preparation). See the [15-idea atlas](09_creative_civic_opportunity_atlas.md), [verified resource catalog](10_public_data_and_repositories.md), and [public criticism/competitive landscape](11_public_voices_and_competitive_gaps.md). A neighborhood evidence brief remains a conservative fallback. These are research-based recommendations, not organizer preferences or guaranteed competitive advantage.

The strongest direct signals are the published rubric's community value and accessibility requirements, meaningful Kiro usage, and the civic challenge materials. Sponsorship alone is weaker evidence of what an individual judge wants. [Official rules][S1]

## 2. Evidence labels

- **Confirmed:** directly stated in a linked primary event or institutional source.
- **Inference — high confidence:** closely follows explicit rubric/challenge language.
- **Inference — medium confidence:** plausible from an institution's published work, without an event-specific scoring promise.
- **Unknown:** not established by the materials reviewed.
- **Proposal:** our own idea, architecture, experiment, or pitch recommendation.

“Partiality” here means observable incentives and institutional priorities. We found no evidence of a secret scoring system, improper favoritism, political litmus test, or a requirement to buy sponsor products beyond the explicit Kiro requirement. A sponsor logo does not establish a judging seat.

## 3. What the Civic Action track actually covers

The organizer-linked [Civic portal][S2] presents four broad opportunities: everyday community life; easier digital government; better neighborhood-service response; and emergency preparedness. Its five data groupings cover public services, community resilience, connected places, college experiences, and education/career opportunity. It explicitly allows new ideas and labels its prompts as guidance rather than final briefs. The [kickoff deck][S3] corroborates the four broad opportunities.

| Area | Our interpretation of a credible product outcome |
|---|---|
| Everyday life and opportunity | A resident/student finds a useful resource and understands the next step |
| Digital government | A specific task becomes easier without inventing requirements or impersonating an agency |
| Neighborhood services | Staff or residents understand a pattern and can take an appropriate follow-up action |
| Emergency readiness | People understand official information and prepare; the product does not invent emergency directions |
| Transportation/public spaces | A commuter or planner understands a disruption, barrier, or evidence-supported intervention |
| College/career pathways | A student compares documented options with clear uncertainty and meaningful constraints |

The word “Civic” is broader than city hall. Campus and opportunity ideas can fit, but tie them to community/public value rather than generic productivity. Ask the track facilitator about borderline ideas.

## 5. Institutional constraints that should shape the concept

### Boston already has relevant technology

Boston's website exposes AI-assisted search. Its Emerging Technology portfolio includes translation, community engagement, transportation and city-data applications. The CityOfBoston GitHub organization hosts **OpenContext**, with an MIT license and documentation for connecting civic data to AI tooling. [S7][S15]

**Freshness update, October 24:** a February 2 [WBZ interview](https://wbznewsradio.iheart.com/content/ai-beta-looks-to-simplify-boston-permitting-process/) described a City AI beta organized around applicants' tasks. On October 21, the City [launched Coastmix](https://www.boston.gov/news/city-boston-launches-coastmix) for coastal-resilience education and community design. These existing products raise the bar for a generic permit chatbot or flood-design interface. Current City leadership also includes CIO Shin-pei Tsay, announced August 13, and Economic Development Chief Emily Knight, with an announced October 19 start; see the [dated stakeholder update](04_recent_boston_priorities_and_data_opportunities.md#3-current-stakeholders-and-what-the-evidence-suggests).

**Our implication:** a generic Boston chatbot is a weak differentiation claim. Pick a completion gap: resolving jurisdiction, assembling a checklist, making output accessible, comparing options, showing evidence freshness, or producing a staff-review packet. OpenContext may be an integration/reference; it is not proof that every needed dataset or write API is available.

### Boston's current employee policy is an adoption constraint

The City's Gen AI policy, version 2.1.0 dated March 6, 2026, keeps employees accountable and bars Gen AI from determining access to benefits/services or evaluating job candidates. It restricts translation of vital documents without Language and Communications Access approval and disallows unapproved external tools for City work. [S16]

**Our implication:** build assistance and human review, using public/synthetic demonstration data. A successful hackathon prototype is not automatically approved for municipal use. A resident-facing independent tool and a City employee tool have different adoption paths. This is a reading of the published policy for product planning, not a claim that the policy is an extra Hackachusetts disqualification rule.

### A linked agent document is a design discussion, not enacted policy

The portal links a Project NANDA document on civic agent identity and authorization. It describes separate resident/agent/operator identities, limited delegation, and accountable actions across services. Its framing favors a trusted access layer rather than one city-owned chatbot. [S17]

**Our implication:** an explicit “what this tool can do” boundary and a visible review/approval record could differentiate an agent prototype. Do not claim that the document is a binding Boston standard, that a proposed identity system is deployed, or that the team has permission to transact with City systems.

### Accessibility is supported by several independent signals

Accessibility is in the judging rubric. The portal links a resource sheet containing document remediation, spreadsheet accessibility, vendor accessibility evidence review, and ASL ideas. That sheet is dated **September 28, 2025** and should be treated as earlier inspiration. It does not prove those ideas won or that the 2026 event mandates them. [S1][S18]

**Our implication:** show one tested access improvement. An automated scanner alone does not establish full accessibility compliance. An ASL translator requires linguistic expertise and validation; it is a poor default one-day promise.

## 6. Candidate ideas and tradeoffs

These earlier concepts remain useful foundations. The [expanded atlas](09_creative_civic_opportunity_atlas.md#how-the-earlier-ideas-evolve) maps each to more distinctive directions and supersedes their earlier ranking. They are original proposals informed by the sources, not official prompts or existing winners. Feasibility assumes a team that can already build a small web app; no numerical win probability is implied.

| Concept | Beneficiary and finished task | Potential strength | Main limitation | Build outlook |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **CivicBridge: one-task resident navigator** | New resident/student prepares a moving-truck parking application and reaches the correct official service | Local, demonstrable, accessible, directly aligned with digital-government prompt | Must beat existing search; exact requirements need authoritative sources | **Strong if narrowly scoped** |
| **Neighborhood Lens: 311 service explorer** | Staff/community group identifies a documented pattern and prepares a follow-up brief | Provided data; transparent analysis; easy to demonstrate | Sample bias; sparse fields; charts alone have limited action value | **Strong fallback** |
| **AccessCheck: municipal document review** | Content editor reviews a few accessibility defects and approves suggested fixes | Clear before/after; concrete inclusion benefit | Full PDF remediation is much larger than it sounds | **Strong only with narrow format/checks** |
| **StepFree Alert: commuter disruption assistant** | Rider sees whether an official disruption affects a selected station/route | Local public API; tangible accessibility use | Missing alerts do not prove accessibility; routing is a separate hard problem | **Moderate** |
| **ReadyTogether: preparedness handoff** | Household gets a source-linked preparation checklist and official referral contacts | Community resilience and language access | Must not invent live alerts, shelter capacity or emergency advice | **Moderate** |
| **Opportunity Map: education/career comparison** | Student compares two documented pathways under stated constraints | Scorecard/IPEDS/O*NET available; equity and workforce fit | Observational data, missing values, crowded recommendation market | **Moderate** |

### A. CivicBridge — earlier resident-navigation hypothesis

**Problem hypothesis:** residents understand their goal but not which agency, form, deadline, and evidence apply. Validate this with a few actual task attempts before expanding scope.

**Demonstration task:** “I am moving in Boston and want to reserve curb space for a moving truck.” The City publishes a [moving-truck parking guide][S19]. A separate [Massachusetts DCR access/moving permit page][S20] shows why jurisdiction can matter. The City page says the moving-truck permit is optional but reserves a legal space; the app must not turn it into a universal legal requirement.

**Minimum complete flow:**

1. Choose the supported municipality/task and provide a few non-sensitive scenario details.
2. Resolve whether the task falls within the supported service; if street ownership is unknown, say so and direct the user to the official contact.
3. Retrieve from a small curated set of current authoritative pages.
4. Produce a short checklist with source links, applicability conditions and last-checked dates.
5. Explain missing information and the exact next step.
6. Let the user review a task packet and open the official application. No live government submission is necessary for the MVP.

**Where AI helps:** translating a user's situation into a structured task, extracting relevant conditions, and explaining the checklist. Keep dates, comparisons and checklist-state transitions deterministic where practical.

**Distinctive demonstration:** change one input so the answer should change; show the relevant source sentence, the changed checklist, and a case where the app declines to invent an answer. Include keyboard completion or a reviewed second-language explanation if time permits.

**Kiro story:** spec encodes source-backed checklist items and unsupported-jurisdiction behavior; steering controls language and claims; tests detect fabricated citations or missing handoffs; a hook runs those checks when service rules change.

**Proposed pilot owner:** campus off-campus housing/student affairs or a community organization helping residents navigate services. This is a target for future validation, not a partner already secured. Expand to another task only after showing the original task works.

**Cut:** citywide autonomous agents, payments, identity verification, access to resident records, comprehensive benefits eligibility, many municipalities, and general web-search answers without a constrained source set.

### B. Neighborhood Lens — data-first fallback

**Minimum flow:** choose service category and area → compare documented request volumes/statuses → inspect source rows and sampling limits → generate a plain-language brief for a staff reviewer.

Use the portal's 311 snapshot and its published query. The displayed selected fields include opening date, category, department, status, neighborhood, ZIP and on-time flag. **They do not include closure time, precise location, free-text narrative, or a stable case identifier in that shown query.** Therefore the starter alone does not support reliable resolution-time analysis, text clustering, or exact duplicate detection. The subsequent [ZIP audit](07_civic_dataset_verification.md) confirmed these omissions and a 5,000-row October 8–18 extract. [S2]

The page labels the saved 311 extract as 5,000 rows while its preview references a larger underlying case universe. Do not label the extract “all Boston complaints.” Request counts measure reporting, not unmet need. Neighborhood comparisons need denominators and compatible geography; demographic correlations are not evidence of an individual person's needs or of causal discrimination.

**Differentiation:** a useful staff-review brief with transparent comparison and missing-data explanation, rather than a second complaint-submission app. [Boston 311 already exists][S21]. AI can explain verified aggregates; it should not decide which residents deserve service.

**Validation:** recompute every displayed statistic independently on a small fixture; check empty categories, dates and denominators. If closure data is unavailable, show status mix or age of currently open records with a clear snapshot time, not average resolution time.

### C. AccessCheck — narrow accessibility review

**Minimum flow:** load a team-created or permitted sample document → detect three supported issues → show the original and proposed correction → reviewer accepts/rejects → export a readable result and review report.

Good first scope: HTML or one constrained document format, with heading order, image descriptions and descriptive links. If choosing PDF, verify that the output preserves text, reading order and document structure; changing appearance alone is not remediation.

Separate deterministic checks from AI suggestions. AI-generated image descriptions require human review. Do not claim full WCAG compliance or “all PDFs fixed.” The older organizer-linked accessibility sheet is an inspiration source, not a certification method. [S18]

**Distinctive demonstration:** show one issue that an automatic checker cannot resolve confidently and the review step that preserves accuracy. That is a better maturity signal than an unjustified green compliance score.

### D. Other concepts: minimum viable boundaries

- **StepFree Alert:** one selected route/station, official alert text and timestamp, plain-language relevance, and an explicit unknown state. Do not promise a safe/accessible alternative route without enough routing and accessibility data.
- **ReadyTogether:** one hazard and locality; official preparation resources and human referral links. Historical FEMA declarations describe the past; they are not live warnings. A referral is not confirmation of shelter availability.
- **Opportunity Map:** compare two or three programs/institutions; show costs, completion and data vintage; distinguish published tuition from net price. O*NET occupations are not vacancies; aggregate earnings do not predict an individual's future.

## 7. Select an idea in 20 minutes

Use this team exercise, not an invented judging formula:

1. **Five minutes:** name the user, their task, current alternative, and one missing capability. Reject ideas whose benefit requires an inaccessible private system.
2. **Five minutes:** open the source data and inspect actual fields. Find one example the demo can complete truthfully.
3. **Five minutes:** sketch input → transformation → useful output → next action. List what fails if AI is wrong.
4. **Five minutes:** compare against the three official criteria and pick a scope all members can explain.

Prefer the concept for which the team can produce the strongest evidence today. A novel architecture without a finished task is a poor trade for this time budget.

### Data checks before implementation

1. Read the actual schema, README, license and collection dates.
2. Distinguish row count, sample size, API pagination and full-population totals.
3. Preserve identifiers and leading zeros; verify units and time zones.
4. Inspect null/suppressed values, geography and duplicate definitions.
5. Test one join by hand. A ZIP, neighborhood, municipality and census block group are different units.
6. Use a documented saved snapshot for reproducibility; show when the app uses it.
7. Keep source URLs and transformation notes beside outputs.
8. Remove unneeded personal identifiers from the demo rather than assuming “open data” means every field is appropriate to republish.

## 9. Proposed product and evaluation design

This is a small-project architecture recommendation, not a requirement to use a particular stack.

```text
Public source pages / licensed data snapshot
    → small validated ingestion layer
    → deterministic filters, calculations and business rules
    → bounded AI extraction / explanation
    → source-linked structured result
    → user or staff review
    → official next step / downloadable packet
```

Build a source record with URL, title, jurisdiction, retrieved date, relevant excerpt and stable internal ID. Generated claims must reference available IDs. An unavailable source should produce an explicit uncertainty state. Keep model credentials server-side and use synthetic residents in examples. A static demonstration dataset is acceptable when accurately described.

### Minimum useful validation

Create 10–15 hand-checked cases, including success, ambiguous intent, unsupported jurisdiction, stale/missing source and malformed input. For the actual concept chosen, measure:

| Measurement | Method | Honest reporting |
|---|---|---|
| Task completion | Can a tester reach the correct next step? | Report numerator, denominator and scenario scope |
| Source accuracy | Check each material output claim against cited source | Separate correct citation from correct interpretation |
| Unsupported-claim behavior | Deliberately ask beyond supported scope | Record whether it defers appropriately |
| Interaction time | Same task using existing source versus prototype | Small convenience sample, not a population-wide effect |
| Accessibility | Keyboard and screen-reader walkthrough plus visual checks | Name tested paths; do not imply complete certification |
| Reliability | Repeat representative cases and test source/API failure | Show failure handling, not only best-case latency |
| Kiro contribution | Trace artifacts to decisions and implemented behavior | Report actual outcomes; don't invent time-saved percentages |

The judges' rubric supports the value of this evidence; the exact test suite above is our proposal. [S1]

## 10. Pitch to the overlapping priorities

Suggested structure:

> “[Specific user] struggles to complete [specific task] because [observed obstacle]. Our prototype helps them [observable outcome] using [named Massachusetts source]. Here is the complete workflow. Here is the evidence and the point where a person reviews the result. Kiro helped us build and verify [specific capability]. We tested [actual sample and result]. Our next validation partner would be [proposed owner], and [clear limitation] remains.”

The common ground is a useful task, local credibility, accessible design, disciplined implementation and a feasible next step. Explain the benefit in the audience's language without making a different or exaggerated promise to each sponsor.

### Likely questions to rehearse

| Question | What a strong answer contains |
|---|---|
| Why does this need AI? | Specific interpretation/explanation work and a comparison to simpler alternatives |
| How is this different from Boston search, 311, or a general assistant? | A tested completion gap and visible feature that fills it |
| Why trust it? | Authoritative sources, dates, bounded scope, explicit unknowns, human review |
| Who will use or operate it? | User, department/community owner, pilot hypothesis, unresolved dependencies |
| What happens when the source changes? | Refresh/version plan, test coverage, visible staleness |
| What did Kiro actually do? | Artifacts and a concrete engineering decision, not a brand mention |
| Can it work outside Boston? | What is reusable versus which rules/data must be replaced |
| Is the impact real? | Measured prototype results separated from projected public benefit |

### Avoidable weak positions

- A statewide “everything assistant” whose only reliable path is a canned answer.
- An AI eligibility verdict presented as an official determination.
- A dashboard that cannot answer what the user should do next.
- A geospatial fairness claim based on incompatible or incomplete datasets.
- A translation or document fix presented as verified without review.
- Sponsor-name decorations or unnecessary AWS/WHOOP integrations presented as scoring advantages.
- A winning-project clone without a new user need or documented improvement.
- A promise of government API access, adoption, or partnership that has not been secured.
