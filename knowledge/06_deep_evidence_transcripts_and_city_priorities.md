# Deeper evidence: City priorities, recordings, awards, and existing products

Checked **October 24, 2026, Eastern Time**. This expands [the current-priorities brief](04_recent_boston_priorities_and_data_opportunities.md) and [past winners](03_past_winners_and_patterns.md). Actual starter-file findings are in [the dataset audit](07_civic_dataset_verification.md).

## 1. What changed after deeper research

The most useful additional evidence is **what Boston has already funded, built, evaluated, and recognized**. Its own Innovation Expo recognized practical improvements to service knowledge, accessibility, procurement, and collection operations. Public recordings also emphasize testing against the existing workflow and involving the people who use it. These are stronger signals of municipal needs than guessing preferences from sponsor logos. They do not establish Hackachusetts judges' private preferences.

**Our recommendation:** choose one resident or staff task, show a completed result, and measure a small improvement honestly. Connect the problem to a dated public statement and the output to actual records. A new chatbot for an already served task needs a specific, demonstrated advantage.

The research now includes three captioned recordings, the City's 34-page Expo poster collection, primary October 23 announcements, and all 22 CSVs in the organizer's Civic starter ZIP. Captions were read as text; this is not a claim that every minute of the videos was watched or the audio independently checked. Full third-party transcripts and large source PDFs are linked, rather than republished here.

## 4. What Boston itself recognized: four actual City award winners

These are **Boston Innovation Expo awards, not Hackachusetts wins**. The [City's award page][E1], updated May 29, names the winners. The [34-page poster collection][E6] was downloaded and text-extracted; the BidBot, disability-access, and mattress posters were also visually inspected to verify chart labels. Page numbers below are one-based PDF pages.

| Project and award | What the evidence says | Our transferable lesson |
|---|---|---|
| **311 Help Center — People's Choice** | [Poster][E2], collection p1: centralized guidance for fragmented service information; reports search time halved, without a detailed evaluation protocol on the poster | Employee knowledge and consistency can be valuable product outcomes |
| **Mapping Disability Access in Boston — Cross-Departmental Collaboration** | [Poster][E3], p24: makes curb ramps, accessible parking and sensory-play resources easier to discover; reports qualitative resident feedback | An access improvement and coordination across departments can be the innovation |
| **Lessons from BidBot — Rising Innovation** | [Poster][E4], p22: procurement assistant grounded in City material. Reports tasks completed 18% faster, scopes of work rated twice as high in quality, and 43% versus 2% believing AI could help their job | Narrow domain, curated evidence, staff involvement and explicit evaluation |
| **New Algorithms for Old Mattresses — Public Impact** | [Poster][E5], p26: replaces inflexible collection zones with balanced daily clustering. Reports 94% less daily pickup imbalance and 31 additional mattresses/day during surges without more vehicle miles | A clear operational bottleneck and a suitable algorithm can outperform a broad interface concept |

The mattress figure is **not** a 94% reduction in cost, distance, or emissions. BidBot's quality figure is a project-reported assessment, not a universal “2× better AI” result. These achievements belong to the original City teams and cannot be represented as our own.

### Stronger evaluation evidence for BidBot

The [BARI 2026 conference abstract][E7], “Evaluating a Generative AI Procurement Assistant in the City of Boston,” describes a randomized field experiment with City procurement employees. It identifies the curated rules/templates corpus and reports quicker completion, more developed documents, and greater confidence. The abstract does not supply enough sample/method detail to independently reproduce the poster's percentages. It nevertheless supports that evaluation was part of the project, rather than just a demo claim. Do not confuse this Boston project with similarly named state procurement products.

## 5. Existing services that change the competitive picture

### Curb Lab: directly relevant to a parking idea

The [current City page][C1] describes open curb data infrastructure and pilots across safety, accessibility, City efficiency and economic development. It links code and identifies the Office of Emerging Technology, Parking Clerk, Streets, New Mobility, and Analytics as participants. The [February launch announcement][C2] identifies partners including Cambridge Systematics, Stamen, CurbIQ/Arcadis, UrbanismNext, and the Open Mobility Foundation. [Stamen's May 22 first-person project account][C3] describes its prototype map, time controls, permit/payment filters, and initially Chinatown-focused coverage.

**Implication:** “AI explains Boston parking” alone is weak novelty. A viable distinct task might be preparing a resident permit application, explaining missing documents, or organizing a dated restriction into a reviewable itinerary. These are hypotheses, not verified user gaps. The starter building-permit table does not contain resident parking permits or authoritative curb rules. This pass did not validate a public production CDS endpoint, full geographic coverage, or availability of parking spaces.

### YouthLine: direct competition for a generic opportunity finder

[YouthLine's official page][C4] currently describes over 700 programs/resources, AI search, age/neighborhood filters, and assisted contact options. It directs users to providers for current details and distinguishes directory navigation from referrals and emergencies. That suggests a narrower completion gap is necessary: a verified preparation checklist or handoff for one opportunity type. Do not claim Boston lacks an AI youth directory.

### Citywide data strategy and DoIT planning

The [public data-strategy page][C5] emphasizes collaboration, access, learning, and useful data practices. Its linked [April 2026 deck][C6] has a “FINAL” filename but internal draft/confidential discussion markings. Treat the public HTML as the firmer institutional statement; the linked deck is supporting context with ambiguous document status, not a binding policy or scoring rubric.

The [published DoIT FY26 plan][C7] names work on constituent services, the Permit Experience Transformation effort, a shared resident entry point, data products, and evaluation. **FY26 is a dated roadmap:** it does not prove every named system is shipped, nor grant access to internal integrations. Our practical inference is to explain where a small prototype fits in an existing service journey and how someone would maintain its sources.

## 6. Stakeholder interests translated into reviewable evidence

This table is our interpretation, not a confirmed judge roster or a prediction of private bias.

| Stakeholder | Evidence they could reasonably inspect | Demo artifact we can prepare |
|---|---|---|
| Resident or community organization | Is the answer understandable, local, and actionable? | One completed task, cited source, printable/exportable result, correction path |
| Frontline service employee | Does it save repetitive work without creating more review burden? | Before/after task steps; one realistic failure and recovery |
| DoIT / Analytics / Emerging Technology | Can the result be traced and evaluated? | Fixed dataset version, deterministic totals, timestamps, test cases and limitations |
| Department owner | Does it fit their actual mandate and handoff? | Proposed pilot owner and integration boundary, clearly marked as unconfirmed |
| Coalition / state innovation ecosystem | Is this a useful Massachusetts application with a plausible next step? | Local problem evidence and a narrowly scoped pilot hypothesis |
| Kiro / AWS | Was Kiro meaningfully used to build and improve the project? | Actual spec → Kiro action → verified behavior, supported by committed `/.kiro` artifacts |
| Hackachusetts judges | Does the submission satisfy the published rubric? | Working demo, original value, clear description and written Kiro account per document 01 |

**Video recommendation remains:** use approximately 20–30 seconds of actual Kiro build evidence inside an under-three-minute demo. Show your work and its effect on the product. This is a presentation recommendation, not an additional rule requiring Kiro screen footage. Use most of the time to demonstrate the product's completed task.

## 7. How to use this research without overstating it

For every claim in the pitch, attach one of four labels in preparation notes: **official requirement**, **source-reported fact**, **our observed result**, or **hypothesis**. Remove the labels from spoken narration only when the wording itself makes the distinction clear.

For example: “The City is reviewing its rodent action plan” is supported by a dated hearing notice. “The supplied file contains 5,000 311 records” is our directly observed audit result. “A community reviewer would find this brief useful” needs user validation. “Our tool reduces City workload by 50%” is unsupported unless we actually measured it under stated conditions.

The next useful research step is a short conversation with a real intended user, after selecting a task. No stakeholder has been contacted and no partnership, endorsement, internal database access, or adoption commitment is established by this research.
